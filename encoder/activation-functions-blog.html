01

Why activation functions exist

A neuron computes a weighted sum and adds a bias — that's a straight line. Chain many of them and the result is still just one big straight line.

Composing linear maps gives another linear map: $W_2(W_1x)=(W_2W_1)x$. No matter how deep, a purely linear network can only draw a hyperplane. The activation function injects a non-linear kink after each layer, and that single bend is what unlocks the rich hypothesis space deep nets are prized for. Without it, your hundred-layer network has the power of linear regression.

🪢

The kink that lets you fold

A flat sheet can only slice space with planes. Add a fold (a non-linearity) and you can wrap around curved decision boundaries.

🚪

A gate per neuron

Each activation decides how much signal passes — fully, partly, or not at all — turning a sum into a decision.

📉

It also shapes gradients

The function's derivative is what flows backward in training. A good activation keeps that gradient alive; a bad one lets it vanish.

Two jobs, one function: the forward shape decides what the neuron outputs; the derivative decides how well the network can learn. Every trade-off below is really about balancing those two.
02

How to read these curves (and why the derivative matters)

Every plot in this guide shows two lines: the function $f(x)$ in teal and its derivative $f'(x)$ in amber.

When you change the input $x$, the network's forward pass walks along the teal curve. When the network learns, gradients are multiplied by the amber curve. Watch for three things as you explore: where the curve saturates (flattens — derivative near 0, learning stalls), whether it is zero-centered (outputs balanced around 0, which speeds training), and whether the derivative ever dies (sits exactly at 0, freezing those neurons).

Vanishing gradient is the recurring villain: when $f'(x)\approx 0$ over a wide region, the backward signal shrinks layer after layer until early layers barely update. Half the functions here were invented specifically to fight it.

05

The classics: squashing functions

The original non-linearities. They squash any input into a bounded range — elegant, interpretable, and prone to vanishing gradients.

06

The ReLU family: cheap, sparse, dominant

ReLU changed deep learning: trivial to compute, no saturation for positive inputs, and gradients that don't vanish on the positive side. Its descendants patch its one flaw — the "dying" negative half.

07

Smooth & modern: the Transformer-era favorites

Replace ReLU's hard corner with a smooth bend and you get better-behaved gradients. These power most modern vision and language models.

08

The shrink family: thresholding by subtraction

Less common in standard nets, these appear in signal processing, sparse coding, and denoising autoencoders. They zero-out or pull-in small values.

09

Output-layer & special cases

Some functions live at the very end of the network, turning raw scores into usable predictions.

Softmax — the multi-class output workhorse

Softmax isn't a single-variable curve; it takes a whole vector of scores (logits) and turns them into a probability distribution that sums to 1. Bigger scores get exponentially more probability. It's the standard final layer for multi-class classification. Drag the logits and watch the probabilities redistribute:

$$ \text{softmax}(z_i)=\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_j e^{z_j}} $$

Softmax playground — 4 class scores → probabilities

Notice it's relative: shifting every logit by the same amount changes nothing, but widening the gaps sharpens the winner toward 1. That "soft" argmax is why it's called softmax.
10

How to choose — conditions & scenarios

There's no universal best. The pick depends on the layer (hidden vs output), the architecture, and the failure mode you're guarding against. A practical playbook:

By layer position

Hidden layers: default to ReLU. If you see dead neurons or want a small accuracy bump, try Leaky ReLU / GELU / Swish. For Transformers and modern LLMs, GELU or SwiGLU-style gating is standard.
Output layer — pick by task: regression → Identity (linear); binary classification → Sigmoid; multi-class classification → Softmax; multi-label → Sigmoid per class.

By architecture

CNNs favour ReLU and its variants (cheap, sparse, work great with conv stacks). RNNs/LSTMs historically use Tanh and Sigmoid for their gates (bounded outputs keep recurrent state stable). Transformers and large language models lean on GELU. Self-normalizing feed-forward nets use SELU to hold activations at zero mean / unit variance without explicit normalization.

By the problem you're fighting

SymptomLikely causeTry instead
Training stalls in deep nets, early layers don't moveVanishing gradient from saturating Sigmoid/TanhReLU, GELU, Swish
Many neurons output 0 foreverDying ReLU (stuck in negative zone)Leaky/Parametric ReLU, ELU
Want zero-centered activations, faster convergenceReLU is non-negative onlyTanh, ELU, GELU
Want self-normalizing FFN, no BatchNormActivations drift in mean/varianceSELU

The honest answer from the references: start with ReLU in the hidden layers, match the output activation to your task, and treat the rest as trial-and-error tuning. The differences between modern smooth activations are usually small accuracy bumps, not night-and-day.
